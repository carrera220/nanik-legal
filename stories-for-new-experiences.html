Preparing your child for something new, with a story

A first day of school, a new baby, a move to a new home, a trip to the dentist. For adults these are events on a calendar. For a young child, they are big unknowns, and unknowns can feel scary. One of the most effective ways to prepare a child is also one of the simplest: tell them what will happen, in a story, before it does.

Why previewing works

Children worry most about what they cannot picture. A story walks them through the new situation step by step, so when the day arrives it feels familiar. Educators often call this a "social story": a short, simple account of what will happen, what the child might feel, and what they can do. It works best when it is about your child, with their name and details they recognize.

What a good "something new" story includes

  • What will happen, in order. The morning, the arrival, the new faces, what comes next. Keep it concrete.
  • How the character might feel. Excited, nervous, a bit of both. This tells your child that mixed feelings are normal.
  • A small plan. A goodbye ritual, a special object in the pocket, a friendly person to ask for help.
  • A warm, realistic ending. Things go well, with a small bump handled. It should not promise that everything will be perfect.

Common "firsts" and how to use a story

  • Starting school or daycare. Read the story for a week or two beforehand. Mention the teacher's name and the real classroom if you can.
  • A new baby. Include the older child's role and show that there is still plenty of love and one-on-one time for them.
  • Moving home. Talk about the new room, what comes with you, and what will stay the same, such as bedtime and your family.
  • Doctor or dentist visits. Describe what the visit will feel like and how it will end. Honest, calm and short works best.
  • Travel or a new babysitter. Tell them who, where, and when you will be back.

If several changes are coming at once, try to space them out. Starting a new school the same month as a move and a new baby is a lot for one small person.

Sample story: Pip's First Day

About a 3-minute read. About starting school. Adapt the name and details for your child.

Pip was a small rabbit with big ears and a brand-new backpack. Tomorrow was his first day at Meadow School.

That evening, Pip could not stop thinking about it. Would the other bunnies be nice? Would he know where to put his coat? Would he remember how to say hello?

"Mama," he said, "my tummy feels fluttery."

His mother smiled and sat beside him. "That is how an excited tummy and a nervous tummy feel when they are both in the same place. Let me tell you what tomorrow will be like."

"First, we will have breakfast, and I will walk with you down the little path to school. There will be a big green door, and your teacher, Miss Hazel, will be waiting there with a kind smile."

"Then you will hang up your coat on a hook with your name on it. You will sit on a soft mat with the other bunnies, and Miss Hazel will read a story. After that, there is painting, and snack time, and playing outside."

Pip's ears wiggled. "And then?"

"And then, before you know it, I will be at the green door again, waiting for you."

Pip thought about this. "What if I feel wobbly?"

His mother took a tiny, smooth pebble from her pocket. "This is a Hello Stone. Put it in your pocket tomorrow. Whenever you feel wobbly, hold it, take a slow breath, and remember that I am thinking of you."

The next morning, Pip walked down the little path with his backpack on and his hand around the Hello Stone. At the green door, Miss Hazel smiled just as his mother had said.

Inside, Pip found the hook with his name. There was a quiet moment when his tummy fluttered. He held his stone, took a slow breath, and a little bunny with floppy ears said, "Do you want to paint with me?"

Pip painted a big orange sun. He laughed at snack time. By the end of the day, he could hardly believe how fast it had gone.

And at the green door, just as promised, there was his mother, waiting with open arms.

"How was it?" she asked.

Pip squeezed his stone, grinned, and said, "I want to go back tomorrow."

The end.

Tips for the days around the event

  • Read the story at bedtime for several nights beforehand, and again the night before.
  • Let your child ask questions, and answer honestly and briefly.
  • Create a small comfort ritual, like the Hello Stone, a note in a lunchbox, or a special goodbye hug.
  • Afterward, ask about one good thing and one hard thing, and then simply listen.
